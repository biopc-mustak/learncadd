1.1 What Is Molecular Docking?

Molecular docking is a computational method that predicts how a small molecule (the ligand) positions itself within a three-dimensional binding site on a larger biological macromolecule (the receptor), and estimates the energetic favorability of that arrangement.

Put simply: docking attempts to answer the question, "If this drug-like molecule approaches this protein, where would it fit, and how well would it fit?"

The process involves two computational tasks performed together:

  1. Sampling — exploring the many possible ways the ligand could position and orient itself in the binding site (called poses).
  2. Scoring — assigning an estimated energy value (the docking score) to each pose, ranking poses from most to least favorable.
🎓
BEGINNER NOTE

Think of a protein as a lock with a uniquely shaped keyhole (the binding pocket). Molecular docking is a simulation of trying thousands of key shapes (ligand conformations) in that lock and scoring how well each one fits. The software then ranks the best fits for you to examine.

1.3 Binding Models: Lock-and-Key vs. Induced Fit

Two classical models describe how ligands bind to proteins:

Lock-and-Key Model

Proposed by Emil Fischer in 1894. The protein's binding site is rigid (the lock), and only a ligand of exactly the right shape (the key) can fit. This model correctly predicts many binding events but underestimates the protein's flexibility.

Standard rigid docking approximates the lock-and-key model: the protein is treated as fixed and only the ligand's pose and conformation are varied.

Induced-Fit Model

Proposed by Daniel Koshland in 1958. When a ligand approaches, the protein's binding site changes shape to accommodate the ligand — the protein adapts to the ligand rather than simply accepting or rejecting it. This better reflects experimental observations, particularly for flexible enzymes.

Flexible docking and ensemble docking attempt to model induced fit by allowing side chains or the entire protein to move during docking.

1.4 Role of Docking in Computer-Aided Drug Design (CADD)

Molecular docking occupies a central position in the drug discovery pipeline. CADD broadly divides into two strategies:

Structure-Based Drug Design (SBDD)

SBDD uses the three-dimensional structure of the target protein (from X-ray crystallography, NMR, cryo-EM, or AlphaFold prediction) to rationally design or screen compounds. Molecular docking is a core SBDD tool. The idea: if you know what the binding site looks like, you can computationally evaluate how well various molecules might bind there.

Ligand-Based Drug Design (LBDD)

LBDD is used when the protein structure is unknown. Instead, it relies on information from known active compounds. Pharmacophore modeling, QSAR (Quantitative Structure–Activity Relationship), and 3D-similarity searches are LBDD methods. Docking is not used directly in LBDD, but the two approaches are often combined.

Hit Identification and Virtual Screening

In early drug discovery, researchers face libraries of thousands to millions of compounds. Experimentally testing all of them is prohibitively expensive. Virtual screening uses docking to rapidly score an entire compound library against a target, ranking candidates by predicted docking score. The highest-ranked compounds (the hits) are then selected for experimental validation.

Lead optimization follows: promising hits are chemically modified to improve potency, selectivity, and pharmacokinetic properties, with docking guiding structural modifications.

1.6 Limitations and Common Misconceptions

🔬
SCIENTIFIC CAUTION — What Docking Cannot Prove

Docking is a computational prediction method, not an experimental measurement. A favorable docking score alone does not prove any of the following:

  • That the ligand actually binds to the protein in a cell or organism
  • That the ligand inhibits or activates the protein's function
  • That the ligand has any therapeutic efficacy
  • That the predicted binding pose is correct
  • That the ligand is non-toxic or pharmacologically suitable

Docking provides a computational priority list to guide experimental validation. It is a filtering and hypothesis-generation tool, not a proof of binding or activity.

MisconceptionScientific Reality
"A score of −10 kcal/mol means the drug will work in humans"Docking scores are not directly comparable to experimental binding constants (Kd or Ki). Many potent docked compounds fail in cell-based or animal studies.
"The best-scoring compound is always the best drug candidate"Drug candidacy depends on ADMET properties (absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, toxicity), selectivity, and many factors docking does not evaluate.
"Rigid docking accurately models all binding events"Proteins are dynamic. Induced-fit effects, conformational selection, and water-mediated interactions are largely ignored in rigid docking.
"An RMSD below 2 Å confirms the docking protocol is valid for all ligands"Redocking RMSD validates the protocol only for the co-crystallized ligand in that specific structure. Cross-docking a different ligand may produce different accuracy.
"Scoring functions accurately predict absolute binding affinities"Current scoring functions are approximations with well-documented shortcomings. They rank poses reasonably well but do not reliably predict absolute binding energies.
"AutoDock Vina is the gold standard for all docking tasks"Vina is excellent and widely validated, but different targets and compound classes may favor different software. No single program is best for all situations.

2.2 Preparation Tools

AutoDockTools (ADT) / MGLTools

AutoDockTools is a graphical interface bundled with MGLTools, developed at Scripps. It is the traditional tool for preparing PDBQT files. Key uses: adding hydrogen atoms, assigning Gasteiger charges, defining rotatable bonds, setting the docking grid box, and generating conf.txt.

  • License: Free for academic use
  • OS: Windows, macOS, Linux
  • Status: Actively used but aging; Python 2 based; some users report installation difficulties on modern Windows
  • Official site: ccsb.scripps.edu/mgltools/

Meeko

Meeko is a modern Python-based ligand preparation toolkit designed for use with AutoDock Vina 1.2+. It is the preferred alternative to AutoDockTools for ligand preparation because it is actively maintained, scriptable, and handles macrocycles better.

  • License: Apache 2.0 (free)
  • Install: pip install meeko
  • Use: Convert SDF or MOL2 to PDBQT; write Vina input from Python
  • Official repo: github.com/forlilab/Meeko

Open Babel

Open Babel is a chemical file format conversion toolkit. It can convert between SDF, PDB, MOL2, PDBQT, SMILES, and many other formats. It can also add hydrogens and perform basic geometry optimization.

  • License: GNU GPL (free)
  • Command line: obabel input.sdf -O output.pdbqt --gen3d -p 7.4
  • GUI: Available via OpenBabelGUI on Windows
  • Official site: openbabel.org

Avogadro

Avogadro is a free molecular editor with a graphical interface. It is useful for building small molecules, visualizing 3D structures, and performing basic energy minimization. Recommended for beginners who want a visual way to inspect and edit ligands before preparing them for docking.

  • License: GNU GPL (free)
  • Official site: avogadro.cc

RDKit

RDKit is a cheminformatics library for Python. Advanced users use it for ligand preparation, descriptor calculation, pharmacophore modeling, and structure–activity relationship analysis. It is not required for basic docking but is widely used in computational drug discovery pipelines.

  • License: BSD (free)
  • Official site: rdkit.org

2.3 Visualization Tools

PyMOL

PyMOL is the most widely used molecular visualization software in structural biology. It is essential for inspecting docking results, measuring interaction distances, generating publication-quality images, and exporting pose data.

  • License: Commercial (Schrödinger PyMOL) or free open-source (PyMOL Open-Source)
  • Academic license: Available at reduced cost from Schrödinger
  • Free version: PyMOL Open-Source, available via conda or pip
  • Official site: pymol.org

UCSF Chimera and ChimeraX

UCSF Chimera is a free molecular visualization program from UCSF. ChimeraX is its modern successor with improved performance and features. Both are widely used for protein structure analysis, superposition, and generating figures.

  • License: Free for non-commercial use
  • ChimeraX site: rbvi.ucsf.edu/chimerax/
  • Recommendation: Use ChimeraX for new work; Chimera is still supported but no longer receiving major updates

BIOVIA Discovery Studio Visualizer

Discovery Studio Visualizer (DSV) is a free molecular visualization application from Dassault Systèmes. It is widely used to generate 2D protein–ligand interaction diagrams showing hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic contacts, pi-interactions, and unfavorable contacts.

  • License: Free (Visualizer only; full Discovery Studio suite is commercial)
  • OS: Windows only
  • Official site: 3ds.com/products/biovia/discovery-studio
  • Key use in docking: Load receptor + docked pose as a complex; run Analyze → Protein–Ligand Interactions to generate 2D interaction diagrams

Understanding the PDB File

Open the downloaded .pdb file in a text editor. It contains several record types:

  • ATOM records — protein backbone and side-chain atoms
  • HETATM records — heteroatoms: ligands, cofactors, metals, water molecules
  • REMARK records — metadata including missing residues, resolution, and structure notes
  • CONECT records — covalent connectivity for HETATM entries
⚠️
DO NOT BLINDLY REMOVE ALL HETATM RECORDS

A common beginner mistake is to delete every HETATM entry. This is scientifically unjustified. Consider carefully:

  • Co-crystallized ligand: Usually removed before docking a new ligand — but keep it for redocking validation
  • Water molecules: Remove most, but waters that form bridging contacts in the active site may be important to retain
  • Zinc or other structural metals: Often critical for protein fold and binding; removing them can destabilize the structure
  • Cofactors (e.g., FAD, heme): May be essential for the protein's function; retaining them is often scientifically justified

Justify your decision for every heteroatom type in your Methods section.

Adding Hydrogen Atoms

PDB files from X-ray crystallography typically lack hydrogen atoms because X-ray diffraction is insensitive to hydrogen. AutoDock Vina requires hydrogen atoms to compute interactions correctly. You must add them before creating the PDBQT file.

  • In AutoDockTools: Edit → Hydrogens → Add → All Hydrogens
  • Via Open Babel: obabel receptor.pdb -O receptor_H.pdb -p 7.4 (adds H at pH 7.4)

Protonation States

The correct protonation of amino acid side chains depends on pH and the local electrostatic environment. At physiological pH (~7.4):

  • Aspartate (Asp) and Glutamate (Glu): negatively charged (deprotonated)
  • Lysine (Lys) and Arginine (Arg): positively charged (protonated)
  • Histidine (His): depends on local environment — often requires careful consideration
  • Cysteine (Cys): normally protonated unless involved in a metal coordination or disulfide

Tools like H++ (h++) or PROPKA can predict protonation states from the structure.

Preparing the Receptor PDBQT with AutoDockTools

  1. Open AutoDockTools (ADT)
  2. File → Read Molecule → select your PDB file
  3. Edit → Hydrogens → Add → All Hydrogens (then Merge Non-Polar)
  4. Edit → Charges → Compute Gasteiger (assigns partial charges)
  5. Edit → Atoms → Assign AD4 type
  6. Grid → Macromolecule → Choose (select your protein molecule)
  7. File → Save → Write PDBQT → save as receptor.pdbqt
🔬
SCIENTIFIC CAUTION — Gasteiger vs. Kollman Charges

Gasteiger charges are partial charges calculated from electronegativity equalization, appropriate for small molecules and commonly assigned to ligands in AutoDock workflows. Kollman charges are united-atom charges used in AMBER force fields, appropriate for protein receptors in AutoDock 4 (not Vina) workflows.

For AutoDock Vina, the scoring function does not explicitly use the partial charges on the receptor the way AutoDock 4 does — Vina uses its own empirical scoring. However, the AutoDockTools receptor preparation workflow assigns atom types that Vina uses for atom-type-based scoring terms. Do not assign Kollman charges to a Vina receptor and claim it is a Gasteiger assignment, and vice versa. Follow the preparation workflow documented for the version and program you are actually using.

Verifying the Receptor PDBQT

Open receptor.pdbqt in a text editor. Each line should look like:

ATOM      2  CA  GLY A   1      11.234  21.567  31.890  0.00  0.00     0.108 C

The last column is the atom type (N, C, A, OA, etc.) used by AutoDock Vina. The second-to-last column is the partial charge. If the file is empty or these columns are missing, the preparation failed.

4.2 Ligand Preparation

Step 1 — Download a 3D SDF Structure from PubChem

  1. Go to pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov
  2. Search for your compound by name, CAS number, or SMILES string
  3. Click on the compound entry
  4. Click Download → SDF → 3D Conformer
  5. Save as ligand.sdf
⚠️
DOWNLOAD 3D — NOT 2D

PubChem offers both 2D and 3D structure downloads. Always download the 3D conformer. A 2D structure has all atoms in a flat plane (all Z-coordinates = 0), which will produce incorrect docking geometry. If a 3D conformer is unavailable, use Open Babel to generate 3D coordinates: obabel ligand_2d.sdf -O ligand_3d.sdf --gen3d

Verifying Chemical Identity and Stereochemistry

Before preparing a ligand, confirm it is the correct molecule:

  • Compare the PubChem CID with published literature values
  • Check stereochemistry: if the compound has chiral centers, confirm you have the biologically relevant enantiomer
  • Verify the molecular formula matches literature
  • Open the SDF in Avogadro or a structure viewer to visually inspect the 3D geometry

Protonation States and Tautomers

Like proteins, small molecules exist in different protonation states and tautomeric forms depending on pH. At physiological pH 7.4, most carboxylic acids are deprotonated and most amines are protonated. Tools to predict the dominant form:

  • Open Babel: obabel ligand.sdf -O ligand_ph74.sdf -p 7.4 (adjusts for pH 7.4)
  • MolConvert (ChemAxon): More sophisticated tautomer and protonation handling
  • pkCSM or SwissADME: Online tools for pKa prediction

Energy Minimization

Energy minimization moves the ligand's atoms to a low-energy geometry by relaxing bond lengths, bond angles, and dihedral angles. Common force fields:

  • MMFF94 (Merck Molecular Force Field): Preferred for drug-like molecules; available in Avogadro and Open Babel
  • UFF (Universal Force Field): Broadly applicable; less optimized for organic molecules
🔬
SCIENTIFIC CAUTION — Energy Minimization and Bound Conformation

Energy minimization produces a low-energy unbound conformation of the ligand — not the conformation it adopts when bound in the protein. The bound bioactive conformation is often not the global minimum energy conformation. AutoDock Vina's docking engine samples multiple conformations during the search, so an approximately correct starting geometry is sufficient. The minimization step primarily corrects grossly distorted bond lengths and angles in computationally generated structures.

Preparing Ligand PDBQT with AutoDockTools

  1. Open AutoDockTools
  2. Ligand → Input → Open → select your SDF or PDB file
  3. AutoDockTools will automatically detect and assign rotatable bonds
  4. Ligand → Torsion Tree → Detect Root (the root is a rigid atom from which the torsion tree branches)
  5. Ligand → Output → Save as PDBQT → save as ligand.pdbqt

Preparing Ligand PDBQT with Open Babel (Command Line)

CMD — Windows
obabel ligand.sdf -O ligand.pdbqt --partialcharge gasteiger -p 7.4

This converts the SDF file to PDBQT format, assigns Gasteiger partial charges, and adjusts protonation for pH 7.4.

Checking Rotatable Bonds

Open ligand.pdbqt in a text editor and count the BRANCH/ENDBRANCH entries — each pair represents one rotatable bond. The number of rotatable bonds directly affects docking speed and conformational sampling. AutoDock Vina's default exhaustiveness handles up to ~10 rotatable bonds well; >15 may require increased exhaustiveness or more computation time.

4.3 Grid Box Configuration

The grid box defines the three-dimensional search space in which AutoDock Vina looks for poses. It is defined by:

  • Center coordinates (center_x, center_y, center_z): The X, Y, Z position of the box center in Angstroms, in the same coordinate frame as the receptor PDBQT
  • Box dimensions (size_x, size_y, size_z): The width, height, and depth of the box in Angstroms
🎓
BEGINNER NOTE — What Is an Angstrom?

An Angstrom (Å) is a unit of length equal to 0.1 nanometers or 10⁻¹⁰ meters. It is the standard unit used to describe molecular distances. A typical C–C single bond is about 1.54 Å long. A small drug molecule might be 10–20 Å across. A grid box of 20×20×20 Å is large enough to enclose a typical small-molecule binding pocket.

Finding the Grid Box Center

Method A — Using the native co-crystallized ligand (most accurate):

  1. Open the protein PDB file in PyMOL
  2. Select the native ligand (HETATM entries, not water) using PyMOL selection: select lig, resn LIG (replace LIG with your ligand's residue name)
  3. In PyMOL command line: get_extent lig — this reports the min/max XYZ coordinates of the selection
  4. Calculate the center: center_x = (xmin + xmax) / 2
  5. Use the resulting X, Y, Z as your grid box center

Method B — Using PrankWeb (online):

  1. Go to prankweb.cz
  2. Upload your PDB file or enter the PDB ID
  3. PrankWeb predicts binding pockets ranked by score
  4. The top pocket's center coordinates can be used as your grid box center

Choosing Box Dimensions

ScenarioRecommended SizeRationale
Known binding site, typical small molecule20×20×20 ÅCovers standard drug-sized binding pockets
Large or elongated binding site25×25×25 Å or asymmetricEnsure the entire pocket is within the search space
Allosteric site (less defined)22×22×22 ÅSlightly larger to allow flexibility in site definition
Blind docking (whole protein)Cover entire protein + 10 Å marginVery slow; use only for site discovery
⚠️
DO NOT USE ARBITRARY BOX SIZES

Some tutorials suggest a universal box size of 60×60×60 Å. This is poor practice. A large box wastes computation time and reduces sampling efficiency within the actual binding site. A box smaller than the binding site will truncate poses. Base your box dimensions on the actual physical extent of the binding pocket, using the native ligand or pocket prediction tool as a guide. Always justify your chosen dimensions in your Methods section.

🔬
SCIENTIFIC CAUTION — exhaustiveness and RAM

exhaustiveness controls the breadth of pose search, not memory allocation in a simple linear way. The relationship between exhaustiveness and actual memory consumption depends on the implementation, the system, and the number of CPU threads used. Claims that "exhaustiveness = 32 uses 4× the RAM of exhaustiveness = 8" are oversimplifications. For most drug-like molecules, the default exhaustiveness of 8 is sufficient; increase to 16–32 for large or flexible ligands, or when you need reproducible results for publication.

5.1 Understanding Directories and File Paths

What Is a Directory?

A directory (also called a folder) is a container on your computer's storage that holds files and other directories. You are already familiar with directories from File Explorer — every folder you open is a directory.

Directories are organized in a hierarchy, starting from a drive letter (such as C:) at the top. This hierarchy is sometimes called a tree because it branches outward from the root.

What Is a File Path?

A file path is the complete address of a file or directory on your computer. It tells the operating system exactly where to find something by listing every directory from the drive letter down to the target. Example:

│  │     └─ Subdirectory: poses (inside the vina folder)

6.3 Understanding and Parsing Docking Results

Reading the Pose Table

After a successful run, Vina prints (and optionally logs) a pose table:

EXPECTED — Vina Pose Table
mode |   affinity | dist from best mode
     | (kcal/mol) | rmsd l.b.| rmsd u.b.
-----+------------+----------+----------
   1 |       -8.5 |    0.000 |    0.000
   2 |       -8.1 |    1.823 |    2.456
   3 |       -7.9 |    2.044 |    3.112

Column meanings:

  • mode — The rank of the pose. Mode 1 has the best (most negative) score. This number is used to identify the pose when splitting with vina_split.
  • affinity (kcal/mol) — The estimated binding free energy for this pose. More negative = more favorable. This is the docking score from Vina's empirical scoring function. It is an approximation, not a measured binding affinity.
  • rmsd l.b. (lower bound) — Root Mean Square Deviation of this pose from Mode 1, calculated using the minimum RMSD matching (accounting for symmetry). A value of 0.000 for Mode 1 is always expected because Mode 1 is compared to itself.
  • rmsd u.b. (upper bound) — RMSD calculated without symmetry matching. These two RMSD columns together indicate how geometrically different each pose is from Mode 1.
🚨
CRITICAL — Vina Output RMSD ≠ Validation RMSD

The rmsd l.b. and rmsd u.b. columns in the Vina output table describe how different each pose is from Mode 1 within the same docking run. They are not the validation RMSD calculated against a crystal structure. Do not report these values as your redocking validation RMSD. For redocking validation, you must calculate the RMSD between your top-ranked docked pose and the co-crystallized ligand's experimental position using PyMOL, ProFit, or a similar tool.

Exporting Results to CSV

To create a CSV spreadsheet from your log file, use this PowerShell script. It is designed to be robust, validate its input, and report clearly when something goes wrong.

PowerShell — Parse Log to CSV (Robust Script)
# Parse-VinaLog.ps1
# Parses a Vina log file and exports the pose table to CSV.
# Usage: Run this script from C:\vina\ after docking.
# Requires: PowerShell 5.0 or later (included with Windows 10/11)

# ── Configuration ───────────────────────────────────────────────
$LogFile    = ".\log.txt"     # Path to your Vina log file
$CsvOutput  = ".\results.csv" # Path for the output CSV file

# ── 1. Verify input file exists ─────────────────────────────────
if (-not (Test-Path $LogFile)) {
    Write-Error "Log file not found: $LogFile"
    exit 1
}

# ── 2. Read the log file ─────────────────────────────────────────
$lines = Get-Content $LogFile

# ── 3. Find the pose table header line ──────────────────────────
# The header contains "affinity" — this marks the start of the table.
$headerIndex = -1
for ($i = 0; $i -lt $lines.Count; $i++) {
    if ($lines[$i] -match 'affinity') {
        $headerIndex = $i
        break
    }
}

if ($headerIndex -lt 0) {
    Write-Error "Could not find pose table in log. Check that docking completed successfully."
    exit 2
}

# ── 4. Parse data lines (skip header and separator lines) ────────
$results = @()
for ($i = $headerIndex + 3; $i -lt $lines.Count; $i++) {
    $line = $lines[$i].Trim()
    if ($line -match '^(\d+)\s+([-\d.]+)\s+([\d.]+)\s+([\d.]+)') {
        $results += [PSCustomObject]@{
            mode            = $Matches[1]
            affinity_kcal_mol = $Matches[2]
            rmsd_lb         = $Matches[3]
            rmsd_ub         = $Matches[4]
        }
    } else {
        break  # Stop at first non-data line after the table
    }
}

# ── 5. Report and export ─────────────────────────────────────────
if ($results.Count -eq 0) {
    Write-Warning "No pose data rows were found. The log may be empty or malformed."
    exit 3
}

$results | Export-Csv -Path $CsvOutput -NoTypeInformation
Write-Host "Exported $($results.Count) poses to $CsvOutput"
$results | Format-Table  # Display table in terminal

How to use this script:

  1. Open Notepad
  2. Copy the script above and paste it into Notepad
  3. Save as Parse-VinaLog.ps1 in C:\vina\ — ensure the extension is .ps1 not .ps1.txt
  4. Open PowerShell in C:\vina\
  5. Run: .\Parse-VinaLog.ps1
  6. If PowerShell reports a script execution policy error, run: Set-ExecutionPolicy -Scope CurrentUser RemoteSigned and try again

Opening the CSV in Microsoft Excel:

  1. Open File Explorer and navigate to C:\vina\
  2. Double-click results.csv — it opens in Excel automatically
  3. Verify four columns: mode, affinity_kcal_mol, rmsd_lb, rmsd_ub
  4. If values appear in a single column, use Data → Text to Columns → Delimited → Comma

Pose Ranking and Structural Plausibility

When evaluating docking results, do not rely on docking score alone. For each top-ranked pose, assess:

  • Location: Is the ligand inside the defined binding pocket, or has it docked to a surface site or outside the protein?
  • Orientation: Does the ligand's functional groups point toward known key residues?
  • Clashes: Does the ligand sterically overlap with any protein atoms?
  • Chemical sense: Do the predicted hydrogen bond donors/acceptors match the protein residues' chemistry?
  • Literature comparison: Do the interacting residues match those identified in published mutagenesis or crystallography studies?

7.2 Redocking Validation

Redocking validation is the standard method for verifying that a docking protocol can reproduce a known binding mode. It is a mandatory component of a rigorous docking study for publication.

Procedure — Step by Step

  1. Select a structure with a co-crystallized ligand: Find a PDB entry for your target that contains a small-molecule ligand in the binding site. High resolution (<2.5 Å) is preferable.
  2. Extract the native ligand: Separate the co-crystallized ligand from the PDB file and save it as a reference pose. Do not modify its coordinates — this is your experimental ground truth.
  3. Prepare the receptor consistently: Remove the ligand from the protein PDB file and prepare it exactly as you do for your main study (same H addition method, same charge assignment, same chain selection).
  4. Prepare the native ligand for docking: Prepare it as you would any ligand (add H, generate PDBQT) but do not change its identity. Keep the same stereochemistry.
  5. Define the grid box: Center the box on the native ligand's position. The ligand defines the binding site — use get_extent in PyMOL on the native ligand.
  6. Run docking: Execute AutoDock Vina with the same parameters you plan to use for your study.
  7. Identify the best-ranked pose: Extract Mode 1 (Pose 1) from the output.
  8. Superimpose and calculate RMSD: In PyMOL, load both the docked pose (Mode 1) and the native ligand. Align the protein structures if needed. Measure RMSD between the docked pose and the native ligand.
  9. Report the validation RMSD: An RMSD <2.0 Å is a commonly accepted criterion for successful pose reproduction.
🔬
SCIENTIFIC CAUTION — What RMSD < 2 Å Does and Does Not Mean

An RMSD of <2 Å means the docking protocol successfully reproduced the co-crystallized binding pose for that specific ligand in that specific structure. It does not mean:

  • The protocol will produce accurate poses for all ligands docked to this target
  • The docking scores accurately predict experimental binding affinities
  • The top-ranked pose for a novel compound is necessarily correct
  • The binding site geometry will be the same for structurally different compounds

Redocking validation is a necessary but not sufficient condition for a reliable docking study. Report the RMSD value precisely and state which crystal structure was used.

⚠️
CALCULATING RMSD — ATOM MAPPING MATTERS

RMSD calculation requires that corresponding atoms are correctly mapped between the docked pose and the reference. For molecules with symmetric groups (e.g., two identical phenyl rings, two carboxylate oxygens), naïve RMSD calculation can produce incorrect values due to mapping different atoms. Use RMSD tools that account for molecular symmetry, or verify atom numbering carefully. PyMOL's align and rms_cur commands and specialized tools like SPYRMSD can handle symmetric molecules appropriately.

Calculating RMSD in PyMOL

9.2 Systematic Diagnostic Procedure

When a docking run fails or produces suspicious results, follow this diagnostic sequence before making changes:

  1. Confirm the working directory. Run cd (CMD) or Get-Location (PS). The result must show your docking project folder.
  2. Confirm all required files exist. Run dir *.pdbqt and dir conf.txt. All three files must be present and non-empty.
  3. Read the configuration file. Run type conf.txt. Verify filenames, coordinates, and parameter values are correct.
  4. Check the Vina version and supported options. Run vina.exe --help (CMD) or .\vina.exe --help (PS). Confirm the version and that your options are listed.
  5. Read the log file. Run type log.txt (CMD) or Get-Content .\log.txt (PS). Look for error messages, the pose table, and the "Writing output... done." line.
  6. Verify the output file. Run dir output.pdbqt. The file size should be greater than zero.
  7. Open in PyMOL. Load receptor.pdbqt and output_ligand_01.pdbqt. Visually confirm the ligand is inside the binding pocket.

Appendix E — Glossary of Molecular Docking and Command-Line Terms

Affinity (kcal/mol)
AutoDock Vina's estimated binding free energy for a given pose. More negative values indicate more favorable predicted binding. This is an approximation, not a measured thermodynamic quantity.
Angstrom (Å)
A unit of length equal to 10⁻¹⁰ meters (0.1 nm). The standard unit for molecular distances. A typical bond length is 1–2 Å.
Atom type
A label assigned to each atom in a PDBQT file that specifies the atom's chemical element and bonding environment. Used by AutoDock Vina's scoring function to calculate interaction energies.
Binding pocket / Binding site
A cavity or groove on the protein surface where a ligand binds. Defined by surrounding amino acid residues that form contacts with the ligand.
CADD
Computer-Aided Drug Design. The use of computational methods to assist in the discovery and development of drug molecules.
CMD (Command Prompt)
The classic Windows terminal application. Available on all Windows versions. Uses an older, simpler command language than PowerShell.
conf.txt
The plain-text configuration file for AutoDock Vina. Specifies all docking parameters: input files, grid box center and size, and search settings.
Conformation
A specific three-dimensional arrangement of a molecule defined by its rotatable bonds. A molecule with 5 rotatable bonds can adopt many different conformations.
Docking score
The numerical output of a scoring function for a given pose. In Vina, expressed in kcal/mol. Lower (more negative) scores indicate more favorable predicted binding.
Energy minimization
A computational procedure that adjusts atomic positions to reduce the molecule's potential energy. Used during ligand preparation to correct geometry.
Exhaustiveness
An AutoDock Vina parameter controlling how thoroughly the docking algorithm searches for poses. Higher values improve sampling but increase computation time.
Gasteiger charges
Partial atomic charges calculated using the Gasteiger electronegativity equalization method. Appropriate for small molecules in AutoDock-based workflows.
Grid box
The three-dimensional search space defined in AutoDock Vina by center coordinates (center_x, center_y, center_z) and dimensions (size_x, size_y, size_z). Vina only places poses within this box.
Hydrogen bond
An electrostatic interaction between a hydrogen bond donor (N–H or O–H) and a hydrogen bond acceptor (electronegative atom with lone pair). Important for ligand selectivity and binding affinity.
Induced fit
A binding model in which the protein's conformation changes to accommodate the incoming ligand. Contrasts with the rigid lock-and-key model.
Ligand
In docking, the small molecule whose binding to the receptor is being simulated. Usually a drug-like compound.
Lock-and-key model
A binding model proposed by Fischer (1894) in which the protein (lock) has a rigid, complementary shape that only matches the correct ligand (key).
Meeko
A Python-based ligand preparation toolkit developed for use with AutoDock Vina 1.2+. Preferred modern alternative to AutoDockTools for ligand preparation.
Mode
A distinct binding pose in the AutoDock Vina output. Mode 1 has the most favorable (most negative) docking score.
Molecular docking
A computational method that predicts how a small molecule (ligand) positions itself within the binding site of a macromolecule (receptor) and estimates the binding energy of that arrangement.
num_modes
An AutoDock Vina parameter specifying the maximum number of distinct binding poses to include in the output. Default is 9.
PDB (Protein Data Bank)
A widely-used file format for three-dimensional molecular structures, and the name of the global repository of experimentally determined structures (rcsb.org).
PDBQT
An extension of the PDB format used by AutoDock and AutoDock Vina. The Q and T stand for charge (Q) and torsion (T). Includes atom types and partial charges; for ligands, also encodes the torsion tree.
Pose
A specific three-dimensional position, orientation, and conformation of the ligand within the receptor's binding site.
PowerShell
Microsoft's modern scripting shell, available in Windows 10 and 11. More powerful than CMD. Requires the .\ prefix to run executables in the current directory.
Receptor
In docking, the macromolecule (usually a protein) into which the ligand is docked.
Redocking validation
The process of re-docking a co-crystallized native ligand back into its experimental protein structure to verify that the docking protocol reproduces the known binding mode.
RMSD (Root Mean Square Deviation)
A measure of the average distance between corresponding atoms in two molecular structures. Used in redocking validation to compare a docked pose with the crystal structure pose. Typical acceptance criterion: <2.0 Å.
Rotatable bond
A single bond in a molecule around which rotation is possible without breaking bonds. AutoDock Vina treats each rotatable bond as a degree of freedom during ligand sampling.
Scoring function
A mathematical formula that estimates the binding energy of a ligand pose. AutoDock Vina uses an empirical scoring function calibrated against experimental binding data.
Semi-flexible docking
A docking mode in which the receptor is kept rigid while the ligand adopts multiple conformations. This is AutoDock Vina's default mode.
Virtual screening
The computational process of docking a large library of compounds against a target and ranking them by predicted binding score, to identify promising candidates for experimental testing.
vina_split
A utility program distributed with AutoDock Vina that extracts individual poses from the multi-pose output PDBQT file into separate files.

Appendix F — Frequently Asked Questions

Can I use AlphaFold structures for docking? ▼

Yes, but with important caveats. AlphaFold structures are predicted by a machine learning model, not determined experimentally. The active site geometry may be inaccurate, especially for regions that are flexible or regulated by post-translational modifications. When using AlphaFold structures, explicitly state this in your Methods, discuss the limitations, and if possible, compare with any available experimental structure. Whenever an experimental structure at reasonable resolution exists in the PDB, prefer it.

What does a docking score of −10 kcal/mol mean? ▼

It means AutoDock Vina's empirical scoring function estimated that the pose has a binding free energy of approximately −10 kcal/mol. This is a favorable score, but it is an approximation. It does not directly translate to a specific Ki or Kd value, nor does it guarantee the compound is biologically active. Many highly-scored compounds fail in experimental validation for various reasons including poor ADMET properties, incorrect protein preparation, or scoring function inaccuracies.

How many poses should I analyze? ▼

For a standard study, examine the top 3–5 poses of each compound. Mode 1 is the highest-ranked and is usually the primary pose reported. If Mode 1 looks structurally implausible (e.g., the ligand is clashing severely or positioned outside the pocket), examine Mode 2 and 3. For redocking validation, the RMSD of Mode 1 is typically reported. For a virtual screening study, Mode 1 is usually the representative pose.

Should I remove all water molecules from the protein? ▼

No — this is a common mistake. Most water molecules in the bulk of the structure should be removed for standard rigid docking, but water molecules that form bridging contacts in the active site may play important roles in ligand binding. For example, some kinase inhibitors bind via water-mediated hydrogen bonds. The scientifically correct approach is to review the literature for your specific target, check the crystal structure for active-site waters, and make a justified decision about each one.

What is the difference between --log and shell redirection for logging? ▼

The --log flag (Vina 1.1.2 only) tells Vina's internal code to write a log file directly. The resulting file is Vina's native log and reliably captures the pose table. Shell redirection (> log.txt 2>&1 in CMD or *> log.txt in PowerShell) captures whatever the program prints to the terminal and redirects it to a file. For Vina 1.2+, shell redirection is the only option. Both methods produce the pose table in the log file. Shell redirection may also capture Vina's banner and progress bar output.

Can I run AutoDock Vina on a ligand with more than 20 rotatable bonds? ▼

Technically yes, but practically very slow and potentially unreliable. Each rotatable bond adds a degree of freedom that Vina must explore. With >15 rotatable bonds, docking becomes slow and the sampling may be insufficient even at high exhaustiveness. For very flexible ligands, consider: (1) increasing exhaustiveness to 32 or higher, (2) restricting some rotatable bonds in the preparation step if chemically justified, (3) using an alternative docking approach designed for high flexibility, or (4) explicitly stating in your paper that high ligand flexibility may limit the accuracy of these predictions.

What is an acceptable redocking RMSD for publication? ▼

An RMSD of <2.0 Å is the most commonly cited threshold for successful pose reproduction. However, this is a convention, not a law. For small rigid ligands, <1.5 Å may be expected. For large, flexible ligands or those with symmetric groups, slightly higher values can still represent good pose reproduction. Report the actual value precisely and cite the crystal structure used. If your RMSD is >2 Å, do not hide this — report it, explain possible reasons, and discuss how it affects the interpretation of your results.

How do I report AutoDock Vina in a manuscript? ▼

Report the full software name, version number, and the primary citation. Example: "Molecular docking was performed using AutoDock Vina (version 1.2.5) [Trott & Olson, 2010; Eberhardt et al., 2021]." Also report all preparation tools with versions: AutoDockTools (version), Open Babel (version), Meeko (version if used). Include the exact docking parameters in the Methods section.

BEGINNER NOTE — Why Can't I Just Use a PDB File Directly in Vina?

AutoDock Vina requires PDBQT format because it needs two pieces of information that standard PDB files do not contain: (1) partial atomic charges (the Q), which the scoring function uses to calculate electrostatic terms, and (2) AutoDock-specific atom types (the T), which map atoms to the correct parameters in Vina's scoring function. The PDBQT file is generated from a PDB file by AutoDockTools, Meeko, or Open Babel.

Converting Between Formats with Open Babel

Open Babel is the universal converter between molecular file formats. Here are the most useful conversions for the docking workflow:

CMD — Open Babel Format Conversions

obabel ligand.sdf -O ligand.pdbqt --partialcharge gasteiger -p 7.4

REM Convert PDB to PDBQT (receptor preparation — basic; prefer AutoDockTools for receptors)
obabel receptor.pdb -O receptor.pdbqt

REM Convert PDBQT back to PDB (for visualization in tools that don't read PDBQT)
obabel output_ligand_01.pdbqt -O pose1.pdb

REM Generate 3D coordinates from a 2D SDF or SMILES
obabel ligand_2d.sdf -O ligand_3d.sdf --gen3d

obabel --version
⚠️
OPEN BABEL RECEPTOR PREPARATION CAVEAT

While Open Babel can convert PDB to PDBQT, it assigns atom types using its own algorithm, which may differ from AutoDockTools' assignments in edge cases (particularly for metals, unusual residues, and cofactors). For the receptor (protein), AutoDockTools or Meeko with explicit AutoDock atom-type assignments is preferred. For ligands, Open Babel with --partialcharge gasteiger is widely used and acceptable.

S2 — Batch Docking Multiple Ligands (PowerShell Script)

For network pharmacology and virtual screening studies, you will often need to dock multiple ligands against the same receptor. Rather than running Vina manually for each one, use a PowerShell script to automate the process.

💡
PRO TIP — Batch Docking Setup

Place all ligand PDBQT files in C:\vina\ligands\ using a consistent naming convention (e.g., compound01.pdbqt, compound02.pdbqt). The script below loops through all PDBQT files in that folder, docks each one, saves the output and log with the compound name, and builds a results summary CSV.

PowerShell — Batch Docking Script
# Batch-Dock.ps1
# Docks all ligand PDBQT files in .\ligands\ against the receptor.
# Saves individual logs and poses; writes summary CSV.
# Usage: Run from C:\vina\ in PowerShell
# Prerequisites: vina.exe, receptor.pdbqt, conf_template.txt (see below)

# ── Configuration ──────────────────────────────────────────────
$VinaExe      = ".\vina.exe"
$ConfTemplate = ".\conf_template.txt"  # conf.txt WITHOUT the ligand= and out= lines
$LigandDir    = ".\ligands"
$OutputDir    = ".\results"
$SummaryCsv   = ".\batch_results.csv"

# ── Create output directory ────────────────────────────────────
if (-not (Test-Path $OutputDir)) {
    New-Item -ItemType Directory $OutputDir | Out-Null
}

# ── Read the template conf (without ligand/out lines) ─────────
$templateContent = Get-Content $ConfTemplate -Raw

# ── Collect results ────────────────────────────────────────────
$summary = @()

# ── Loop over all ligand PDBQT files ──────────────────────────
$ligands = Get-ChildItem "$LigandDir\*.pdbqt"
if ($ligands.Count -eq 0) {
    Write-Error "No PDBQT files found in $LigandDir"
    exit 1
}

Write-Host "Found $($ligands.Count) ligands. Starting batch docking..."

foreach ($lig in $ligands) {
    $name    = $lig.BaseName
    $outFile = "$OutputDir\${name}_out.pdbqt"
    $logFile = "$OutputDir\${name}_log.txt"
    $confFile= "$OutputDir\${name}_conf.txt"

    # Build a conf.txt for this ligand
    $conf = $templateContent + "`nligand = $($lig.FullName)`nout = $outFile"
    $conf | Set-Content $confFile

    Write-Host "Docking: $name ..." -NoNewline

    # Run Vina and capture output
    & $VinaExe --config $confFile *> $logFile

    # Parse best score from log
    $bestScore = "N/A"
    $logLines  = Get-Content $logFile -ErrorAction SilentlyContinue
    foreach ($line in $logLines) {
        if ($line -match '^[\s\|]*1[\s\|]+([-\d.]+)') {
            $bestScore = $Matches[1]
            break
        }
    }

    Write-Host " Best score: $bestScore kcal/mol"
    $summary += [PSCustomObject]@{
        Compound  = $name
        BestScore = $bestScore
        LogFile   = $logFile
        OutFile   = $outFile
    }
}

# Write summary CSV
$summary | Export-Csv -Path $SummaryCsv -NoTypeInformation
Write-Host "`nBatch complete. Summary: $SummaryCsv"
$summary | Sort-Object BestScore | Format-Table

S4 — ADMET Screening and Drug-Likeness Filters

After docking, top-ranked compounds should be evaluated for drug-likeness and predicted pharmacokinetics before they are reported as promising candidates. ADMET stands for Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, Excretion, and Toxicity.

Lipinski's Rule of Five (Ro5)

Proposed by Christopher Lipinski (1997) based on analysis of FDA-approved oral drugs, the Rule of Five filters for oral bioavailability:

ParameterCriterionChemical Meaning
Molecular weight (MW)≤ 500 DaLarge molecules absorb poorly across membranes
Hydrogen bond donors (HBD)≤ 5Excess H-bond donors reduce membrane permeability
Hydrogen bond acceptors (HBA)≤ 10Excess H-bond acceptors reduce membrane permeability
Calculated logP (cLogP)≤ 5High lipophilicity indicates poor aqueous solubility

A compound violating two or more of these rules is likely to have poor oral bioavailability. Note: the Rule of Five was derived from oral drugs — it does not apply to IV drugs, natural products, or antibiotics, and violations do not automatically disqualify a compound.

S5 — Extended Troubleshooting Reference

🚨
ERROR — Vina exits silently with no output and no error message

Meaning: The process started but terminated abnormally, often before writing any output.

Most likely causes:

  • Corrupted or truncated PDBQT input file
  • File encoding issue (file saved with BOM or non-ASCII characters in a path)
  • Insufficient available system memory for a very large receptor

Diagnostic:

PowerShell — Check Exit Code
.\vina.exe --config conf.txt *> .\log.txt
Write-Host "Exit code: $LASTEXITCODE"
Get-Content .\log.txt | Select-Object -First 20

Fix: Regenerate both PDBQT files from scratch. Ensure the project path contains no spaces or special characters. Try moving everything to C:\vina\ (a simple, short path).

⚠️
ERROR — All docked poses have scores between 0 and +5 kcal/mol (positive scores)

Meaning: Vina found no favorable poses. All generated poses are energetically unfavorable.

Most likely causes:

  • Grid box is not positioned over the binding site — verify center coordinates carefully
  • Receptor was prepared with errors — severe steric clashes due to missing hydrogens or wrong atom types
  • Ligand PDBQT contains a 2D (flat) structure — Z-coordinates all zero
  • Box is so small that no valid pose fits inside

Diagnostic check in PyMOL:

PyMOL
load receptor.pdbqt
load output.pdbqt, poses
# Check: are poses inside the protein, or floating far away?
zoom receptor
⚠️
ERROR — PowerShell script execution is disabled on this system

Message: cannot be loaded because running scripts is disabled on this system

Meaning: PowerShell's execution policy prevents unsigned scripts from running.

Fix: Run the following in PowerShell as Administrator (or for the current user only):

PowerShell — Enable Script Execution (Current User)
Set-ExecutionPolicy -Scope CurrentUser -ExecutionPolicy RemoteSigned

RemoteSigned allows locally-created scripts to run. It does not affect internet-downloaded scripts, which still require a digital signature. This is the recommended setting for research use. After running this command, retry your script.

⚠️
ERROR — Output PDBQT file contains only the header, no ATOM records

Meaning: Vina wrote the output file but it contains no pose data — typically just copyright/header text.

Most likely causes:

  • Grid box was set so small that no pose fit — increase size_x/y/z
  • energy_range is very small — all poses fell outside the range
  • Ligand has too many rotatable bonds for the exhaustiveness setting

Fix: Temporarily increase box size to 25×25×25 Å and exhaustiveness to 16, then rerun. If poses appear, the original box was too restrictive.

⚠️
ERROR — Discovery Studio shows no interactions in the 2D diagram

Meaning: Discovery Studio did not detect interactions meeting its geometric criteria.

Most likely causes:

  • The ligand was not recognized as a small molecule — it may have been assigned as part of the protein
  • The ligand's residue name in the PDB/PDBQT file is not recognized
  • The complex file contains only one chain with no ligand separation

Fix: In Discovery Studio, expand the structure hierarchy. The ligand should appear as a separate entity under "Ligands." If it appears under "Protein," use Structure → Define as Ligand after selecting it. Alternatively, regenerate the complex PDB from PyMOL ensuring the ligand is a separate object before merging.

S6 — Writing the Discussion Section — Scientific Framing Guide

The Discussion section is where you interpret your results in the context of existing knowledge. It is the most difficult section to write but critically important for demonstrating scientific rigor. This guide provides a framework specific to molecular docking studies.

Structure of a Strong Docking Discussion

  1. Restate the key finding briefly. One sentence: "Compound X showed the most favorable predicted binding affinity (−Y kcal/mol) against [target] among the [N] compounds evaluated."
  2. Interpret the interactions biologically. Explain why the interactions identified matter: "The hydrogen bond with Asp85 is consistent with known interactions required for inhibitor binding at this site, as reported in [citation]."
  3. Compare with known compounds or controls. "Compound X outperformed [reference drug] (−8.1 vs −7.6 kcal/mol), suggesting potentially stronger binding; however, docking scores are not directly comparable to experimental IC50 values."
  4. Connect to structure–activity relationships (SAR) if applicable. "The hydroxyl group at position 3 of Compound X appears to mediate a critical hydrogen bond with Thr180, while Compound Y, lacking this group, showed a 1.4 kcal/mol lower affinity."
  5. Explicitly state limitations. This is mandatory. At minimum: "These results are computational predictions based on a rigid receptor model. Protein flexibility, induced-fit effects, water-mediated interactions, and solvation effects were not fully accounted for. Experimental validation is required."
  6. Propose future experiments. "Future work should include in vitro binding assays (surface plasmon resonance or isothermal titration calorimetry), cell-based enzyme inhibition assays, and molecular dynamics simulation to assess binding stability."
🚨
CRITICAL — Statements to Never Write in a Docking Paper
  • ❌ "Compound X binds strongly to [target]" — docking predicts, not proves binding
  • ❌ "These results confirm that Compound X inhibits [target]" — docking does not confirm inhibition
  • ❌ "Compound X can be used as a drug to treat [disease]" — docking alone cannot support this claim
  • ❌ "The docking score of −10 kcal/mol corresponds to a Ki of X nM" — this conversion is not valid for Vina scores
  • ❌ Reporting only the best-scoring compound without mentioning others
  • ❌ Omitting the redocking validation RMSD from the Results section

S8 — Reproducibility and Record-Keeping

Scientific reproducibility requires that another researcher could repeat your docking study and obtain the same (or statistically equivalent) results. The following practices are essential.

Setting a Fixed Random Seed

AutoDock Vina uses a random starting point for its Monte Carlo search. By default, the seed is chosen randomly, meaning two identical runs may give slightly different results. For exact reproducibility, set a fixed seed in conf.txt:

conf.txt — Add Fixed Seed
seed = 12345    # Any integer; use the same number for reproducible results
                  # Report this value in your Methods section

Report the seed value in your Methods section: "A random seed of 12345 was used for reproducibility."

What to Archive for Reproducibility

  • Original PDB file (downloaded from RCSB with the exact PDB ID and version)
  • Processed receptor.pdbqt and all ligand.pdbqt files
  • conf.txt (complete, with all parameters)
  • output.pdbqt (all poses)
  • Log file from every docking run
  • Python/PowerShell scripts used for any post-processing
  • Software version list (OS version, Vina version, Open Babel version, Python version if applicable)
  • ADMET input/output data
  • PyMOL session files (.pse) for all published figures
💡
PRO TIP — Use a Free Repository for Data Sharing

Many journals now require or recommend sharing computational docking data. Deposit your files in a free public repository before submission:

  • Zenodo — Free, CERN-hosted, generates DOIs
  • Figshare — Free, up to 20 GB private + unlimited public
  • OSF (Open Science Framework) — Free, integrates with many workflows

Deposit your PDBQT files, conf.txt, log files, and any scripts, then cite the repository DOI in your paper under "Data Availability."

🔬
SCIENTIFIC CAUTION — Comparing Scores Across Different Targets

Docking scores from different receptors are not directly comparable in absolute terms. A score of −9.0 kcal/mol against Target A and −8.0 kcal/mol against Target B does not mean the compound binds Target A twice as strongly. Scoring functions are calibrated empirically and their absolute values depend on the target, the pocket shape, and the number of protein atoms surrounding the ligand. Comparisons within the same target (between different ligands) are more meaningful than cross-target comparisons.

Advanced Topic 3 — Molecular Dynamics as a Follow-Up to Docking

Molecular docking treats the protein as rigid. Molecular Dynamics (MD) simulation is the logical next step: it models the motion of all atoms in the protein–ligand complex over time, accounting for protein flexibility, explicit solvent, and thermodynamic equilibration. MD is used to:

  • Confirm that a docked pose is stable over time (not just a transient high-energy contact)
  • Observe induced-fit effects — how the protein rearranges to accommodate the ligand
  • Calculate binding free energy using methods like MM-PBSA or MM-GBSA
  • Identify key residues contributing to binding stability
🎓
BEGINNER NOTE — Is MD Simulation Required for Every Docking Study?

No. MD simulation is optional for basic docking studies and significantly more complex to perform. It requires specialized software (GROMACS, AMBER, NAMD), parameterization of the ligand for the force field, and significantly more computing time. However, many high-impact journals now expect MD as a follow-up for the best-scoring compounds in network pharmacology papers. If you are new to MD, master docking first, then learn MD as a separate skill.

25 Additional Frequently Asked Questions

Answers to the questions most commonly asked by students and researchers new to molecular docking.

Can I dock RNA or DNA instead of protein? ▼

AutoDock Vina can technically dock small molecules to nucleic acid structures if the PDBQT is prepared correctly, but its scoring function was parametrized primarily on protein–ligand data. Results for RNA/DNA targets may be less reliable. Specialized tools like rDock or AutoDock-GPU with custom parameters are better suited for nucleic acid docking. If you use Vina for RNA/DNA, explicitly acknowledge this limitation.

What is the maximum number of atoms in a ligand that Vina can handle? ▼

AutoDock Vina has no hard-coded maximum atom count, but performance degrades significantly with very large ligands (>100 heavy atoms or >20 rotatable bonds). For most drug-like molecules (MW <500 Da, <10 rotatable bonds), Vina performs well. For peptides, macrocycles, or large natural products, consider specialized software or significantly increased exhaustiveness.

Should I use the monomer or dimer for docking a dimeric protein? ▼

It depends on the biology. If the binding site is entirely within one chain (monomer), use the monomer. If the binding site is at the dimer interface (e.g., some HIV protease inhibitors), you must use the dimer. Check the PDB biological assembly information and the literature for your target. The PDB entry page under "Biological Assembly" shows the physiologically relevant form.

What does "flexible receptor" docking mean and when do I need it? ▼

Flexible receptor docking allows selected protein side chains to rotate during the docking search, partially modeling induced-fit effects. AutoDock Vina 1.2+ supports this via the --flex option, which takes a PDBQT file of flexible residues separate from the rigid receptor. Use it when: the binding site contains a known flexible gatekeeper residue, the target has well-documented induced-fit behavior, or standard rigid docking produces poor poses for known binders. It significantly increases computation time.

I have a metal ion (e.g., Zn²⁺) in the active site. How should I handle it? ▼

Metal ions in the binding site are tricky because Vina's scoring function was not designed with explicit metal coordination in mind. For zinc metalloenzymes (e.g., carbonic anhydrase, matrix metalloproteinases), retain the zinc ion in the receptor PDBQT and ensure it has the correct AutoDock atom type (Zn). Some researchers add dummy atoms or use a modified scoring approach. Always note in your Methods that standard Vina scoring may not fully capture metal coordination geometry, and interpret results involving metal-coordinating ligands with extra caution.

My ligand has a charged group (e.g., carboxylate or quaternary amine). Does Vina handle charges correctly? ▼

Vina's empirical scoring function uses atom types that implicitly encode some electrostatic character (e.g., OA = H-bond acceptor oxygen, NA = H-bond acceptor nitrogen). Explicit electrostatic calculations with partial charges are part of AutoDock 4's scoring, not Vina's. Gasteiger charges assigned during preparation are stored in the PDBQT file, but Vina uses them for atom-type classification rather than direct electrostatic terms. Charged groups that form salt bridges or ionic interactions will be partially captured through H-bond and electrostatic atom-type interactions, but the treatment is approximate compared to force-field-based methods.

What is the Vinardo scoring function (available in Vina 1.2+)? ▼

Vinardo is an alternative scoring function developed by Quiroga and Villarreal (2016) and integrated into AutoDock Vina 1.2+. It was specifically optimized for performance on certain target classes and may outperform the classic Vina scoring function for some systems. Enable it by adding scoring = vinardo to your conf.txt (Vina 1.2+ only). The literature comparison between Vina and Vinardo performance is mixed — test both on your system if rigorous comparison is needed.

Can I run AutoDock Vina on a Linux server or cloud computer? ▼

Yes — AutoDock Vina has Linux and macOS binaries available from the same GitHub releases page. The command syntax is identical, except you use ./vina (note: no .exe extension) and may need to set execute permissions: chmod +x vina. Running on a Linux server or HPC cluster is recommended for large virtual screening studies. Cloud options include Google Colab (free, limited) and AWS/Azure instances.

How do I dock a natural product with multiple ring systems and few rotatable bonds? ▼

Rigid, fused-ring natural products (e.g., steroids, terpenoids) with few rotatable bonds are well-suited for Vina. The docking is fast and sampling is thorough even at default exhaustiveness. The main challenge is accurate 3D structure generation — if PubChem does not have a 3D conformer for your natural product, generate it with Avogadro using MMFF94 optimization, or use a web tool like CORINA (commercial) or the free CORINA Classic online tool. Always verify the stereochemistry against the literature structure.

What is the difference between binding affinity (Kd/Ki) and docking score? ▼

Binding affinity (Kd or Ki) is an experimentally measured thermodynamic quantity — it quantifies how tightly a ligand binds to a protein under defined conditions. The docking score is a computational approximation produced by an empirical scoring function. While more negative scores generally correlate with stronger binding within a congeneric series (compounds with the same scaffold), the absolute score values cannot be reliably converted to Kd or Ki. The correlation between Vina scores and experimental affinities is typically modest (R² often 0.3–0.6 across diverse compound sets). Never state that a docking score of −X kcal/mol implies a specific Kd value.

I get different results each time I run Vina with the same input. Is this normal? ▼

Yes, this is expected behavior. AutoDock Vina uses a stochastic Monte Carlo algorithm with a randomly chosen starting seed. Different seeds explore different regions of pose space, leading to slightly different results between runs. At sufficient exhaustiveness (≥16 for most drug-like ligands), the top-ranked mode should be consistent. For exact reproducibility, set a fixed seed in conf.txt: seed = 42 (or any integer). Report the seed in your Methods section.

How do I handle a protein with an N-terminal signal peptide or transmembrane domain? ▼

Signal peptides are cleaved in mature proteins and are usually absent from crystal structures deposited in the PDB (the PDB entry uses the mature, processed sequence). Transmembrane domains are almost never resolved in standard crystal structures due to the difficulty of crystallizing membrane proteins. If your target is a membrane protein, use a structure solved in a detergent or lipid environment if available, or use a homology model. The standard docking workflow applies, but be aware that the physiological membrane environment is not modeled and may affect binding site geometry.

Can I use homology models for docking? ▼

Yes, homology models can be used when no experimental structure is available, but with caveats. The reliability of docking results is strongly dependent on model quality, particularly in the binding site region. Only use a homology model if the template sequence identity is ≥30% (ideally ≥50%), the binding site is well-modeled (verify by comparison with structurally related proteins), and no experimental structure is available. Explicitly state in your Methods that a homology model was used, cite the modeling software (MODELLER, Swiss-Model, etc.), and discuss model quality limitations.

What is the difference between docking score and binding free energy? ▼

Binding free energy (ΔG_bind) is the thermodynamically rigorous quantity describing the free energy change when a ligand binds to a protein. It can be calculated by expensive methods like FEP (Free Energy Perturbation) or MM-PBSA/GBSA. The docking score is a rapid empirical approximation — it estimates binding energy in kcal/mol using a simplified scoring function. Both share the same unit, but docking scores are approximations with well-documented limitations, particularly for absolute affinity prediction. MM-PBSA calculations from MD trajectories provide better estimates of ΔG_bind but require significantly more computation.

My protein has an allosteric site. How do I dock to it? ▼

Allosteric docking is straightforward in terms of the technical procedure — you simply define the grid box centered on the allosteric site rather than the orthosteric (active) site. The challenge is identifying and justifying the allosteric site. Use: (1) literature reporting allosteric sites for your target, (2) PDB structures containing allosteric ligands, (3) binding site prediction tools like PrankWeb, CASTp, or FTMap that can identify secondary pockets. Allosteric sites are often more hydrophobic and less defined than active sites, so a larger grid box (25×25×25 Å) is often appropriate.

Complete Docking & Bioinformatics Glossary (60+ Terms)

A comprehensive reference of every technical term used in this guide and in molecular docking literature.

ADMET
Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, Excretion, Toxicity — the five pharmacokinetic properties used to predict whether a drug candidate will be safe and effective in vivo.
Allosteric site
A binding site on a protein that is distinct from the active (orthosteric) site. Ligands that bind here modulate protein function by inducing conformational changes rather than directly blocking the active site.
AutoDock 4 (AD4)
An older molecular docking program from Scripps Research that uses a force-field-based scoring function. AutoDock Vina is the successor; Vina 1.2+ can optionally use the AD4 scoring function.
B-factor (Temperature factor)
A value in a PDB file indicating the atomic displacement or thermal motion. High B-factor regions are more flexible and their coordinates less reliable — important when evaluating binding site residue quality.
Betweenness centrality
A network analysis metric measuring how often a node (target or compound) lies on the shortest path between other nodes. High betweenness nodes act as hubs or bridges in the network.
Binding free energy (ΔG_bind)
The thermodynamic quantity describing the energy change when a ligand binds to its receptor under standard conditions. Related to Kd by: ΔG = RT ln(Kd). More negative = tighter binding.
Blind docking
Docking in which the grid box covers the entire protein surface, used to discover unknown binding sites. Slower and less precise than site-specific docking.
CASTp
Computed Atlas of Surface Topography of proteins — a web server that identifies and measures surface pockets and cavities in proteins. Used to identify and characterize binding sites.
Chain
In a PDB file, a chain is a continuous polypeptide sequence labeled by a single letter (A, B, C…). Multi-chain PDB files contain multiple protein copies, often as biological assemblies (dimers, tetramers).
ChEMBL
A large manually curated database of bioactive molecules with drug-like properties and their associated pharmacological activity data. Useful for finding known inhibitors for comparison.
Chiral center
An atom (usually carbon) bonded to four different groups, existing in two non-superimposable mirror-image forms (enantiomers: R and S). The correct stereoisomer must be used for docking biologically relevant compounds.
Co-crystallized ligand (native ligand)
A small molecule that was present during protein crystallization and is found bound in the active site in the crystal structure. Used as the reference pose for redocking validation.
Cofactor
A non-protein molecule required for a protein's catalytic activity. Examples: FAD, NAD+, heme, metal ions. May need to be retained in the receptor during preparation.
Covalent docking
Docking in which the ligand forms a covalent bond with a specific protein residue. Requires specialized software (CovDock, AutoDock-GPU with covalent mode) since standard Vina does not model covalent bonds.
Cryo-EM (Cryo-Electron Microscopy)
An experimental method for determining protein structures by imaging flash-frozen samples with an electron beam. Increasingly used for large complexes and membrane proteins. Resolutions are improving rapidly (<2 Å is now achievable).
Degree (network)
In network analysis, the number of edges (connections) a node has. High-degree nodes in a compound–target network are "hub" targets interacted with by many compounds.
Dielectric constant
A measure of a material's ability to screen electrostatic interactions. In implicit solvent models, the protein interior uses a low dielectric (ε ≈ 2–4) and solvent a high one (ε ≈ 80 for water).
DrugBank
A comprehensive database combining chemical, pharmacological, and pharmaceutical data on FDA-approved and experimental drug molecules. Useful for drug repurposing studies.
Energy minimization
A computational procedure that adjusts atomic coordinates to minimize the molecule's potential energy. For ligands, it corrects geometric distortions before docking. Does not necessarily produce the biologically relevant conformation.
Ensemble docking
A docking strategy that uses multiple protein conformations (from MD trajectories or multiple crystal structures) to account for receptor flexibility. Each ligand is docked to multiple receptor conformations.
FEP (Free Energy Perturbation)
A rigorous but computationally expensive method to calculate binding free energies from MD simulations. Considered a gold standard for relative binding affinity prediction but requires significant expertise and GPU resources.
Force field
A mathematical description of the potential energy of a molecular system as a function of atomic coordinates. Examples: MMFF94, AMBER, CHARMM, OPLS. Used for energy minimization and molecular dynamics.
GeneCards
A web database integrating information about human genes including their protein products, disease associations, and expression patterns. Used in network pharmacology to identify disease-associated targets.
GROMACS
A widely used open-source molecular dynamics package. Commonly used as the MD engine following molecular docking to assess binding stability of docked poses.
HETATM
A PDB file record type for heteroatoms — atoms belonging to non-standard residues: small molecule ligands, cofactors, metals, modified amino acids, and water molecules.
Hit compound
In drug discovery, a compound identified by screening (virtual or experimental) that shows activity against a target. Hits are further developed into leads through medicinal chemistry optimization.
Hydrophobic effect
The tendency of non-polar molecules to aggregate in aqueous solution to minimize their contact with water. The hydrophobic effect drives binding of non-polar ligand regions into hydrophobic protein pockets.
IC50
Half maximal inhibitory concentration — the concentration of a compound required to inhibit a biological process by 50%. An experimental measurement, not predictable directly from docking scores.
Ki (Inhibition constant)
A thermodynamic measure of inhibitor potency equal to the dissociation constant for the enzyme-inhibitor complex. Smaller Ki = tighter binding. Related to IC50 by the Cheng-Prusoff equation.
Kd (Dissociation constant)
The equilibrium constant for dissociation of a receptor-ligand complex. Smaller Kd = tighter binding. Related to binding free energy by ΔG = RT ln(Kd).
Lead compound
A drug candidate that has been optimized from a hit compound and shows acceptable potency, selectivity, and preliminary pharmacokinetic properties. The focus of medicinal chemistry development.
Lipinski's Rule of Five
Empirical guidelines for oral bioavailability: MW ≤500, HBD ≤5, HBA ≤10, cLogP ≤5. Compounds violating ≥2 rules are likely to have poor oral absorption.
LogP
The logarithm of the octanol-water partition coefficient — a measure of a compound's lipophilicity. High logP indicates high lipophilicity and may correlate with poor aqueous solubility.
Meeko
A Python-based ligand preparation toolkit for AutoDock Vina 1.2+. Preferred modern alternative to AutoDockTools for ligand PDBQT generation. Supports macrocycles and SMILES input.
MM-PBSA / MM-GBSA
Molecular Mechanics Poisson-Boltzmann/Generalized Born Surface Area — post-MD methods for estimating binding free energy. More accurate than docking scores but require MD trajectories.
MMFF94 (Merck Molecular Force Field 94)
A force field developed by Halgren (1994–1996) at Merck Research Laboratories. Well-parametrized for drug-like organic molecules; preferred for ligand energy minimization before docking.
Monte Carlo sampling
A stochastic optimization algorithm used by AutoDock Vina to explore the pose space. Random moves in position, orientation, and torsion are accepted or rejected based on a Metropolis criterion.
Network pharmacology
A systems biology approach that studies drug action through the lens of complex biological networks (gene–target–pathway–disease networks) rather than the traditional one-drug-one-target paradigm.
NMR (Nuclear Magnetic Resonance)
An experimental method for determining protein structures in solution. NMR structures are typically deposited as ensembles of multiple conformations. For docking, select a single representative conformation (usually Model 1).
Orthosteric site
The primary functional binding site of a protein — the active site for enzymes, the substrate-binding site for receptors. Contrasted with allosteric sites.
PATH (environment variable)
A system environment variable listing directories that the OS searches for executable files. If vina.exe is not in the PATH, you must run it using its full path or from its containing directory.
Pi–pi stacking
An attractive interaction between two aromatic ring systems. Face-to-face (parallel) stacking and edge-to-face (T-shaped) stacking are both energetically favorable and commonly observed in protein–ligand complexes.
PrankWeb
A web server implementing the P2Rank machine learning algorithm for ligand-binding site prediction from protein structure. Useful for identifying the grid box center when no native ligand is available.
ProTox
An online web server for predicting the toxicity of small molecules. ProTox-3.0 predicts acute oral toxicity, hepatotoxicity, cytotoxicity, mutagenicity, and immunotoxicity.
PyMOL
A widely-used molecular visualization system for proteins, nucleic acids, and small molecules. Available as a commercial version (Schrödinger) and an open-source version. Essential for visualizing docking results.
QSAR (Quantitative Structure–Activity Relationship)
A computational approach that correlates molecular descriptors (structural features) of compounds with their biological activities. Part of ligand-based drug design; complements structure-based approaches like docking.
R-factor / R-free
Statistical measures of agreement between a crystallographic model and the experimental diffraction data. R-free below ~0.25 and R-factor below ~0.20 are typical indicators of good model quality.
Resolution (crystallographic)
In X-ray crystallography, the minimum resolvable distance between atoms in Å. Lower values indicate higher resolution and more reliable atomic positions. <2.5 Å is preferred for ligand-binding studies.
Salt bridge
An electrostatic interaction between oppositely charged ionizable groups: a positively charged amine (Lys, Arg) and a negatively charged carboxylate (Asp, Glu). Typically 2.5–4.0 Å between charged atoms.
SBDD (Structure-Based Drug Design)
Drug design approach that uses the three-dimensional structure of the target protein to guide compound selection and optimization. Molecular docking is the core computational tool in SBDD.
Scoring function
A mathematical function that estimates the binding energy (or ranking) of a ligand pose. Types include: empirical (Vina), force-field-based (AutoDock 4), knowledge-based (PMF), and machine-learning-based (GNINA).
Semi-flexible docking
Default AutoDock Vina mode: the protein is treated as rigid while the ligand is fully flexible (all rotatable bonds sampled). The most common mode for drug discovery applications.
SMILES (Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry System)
A linear text notation encoding molecular connectivity and stereochemistry. Example: quercetin SMILES = O=C1C(=C(Oc2cc(O)cc(O)c21)c1ccc(O)c(O)c1)O. No 3D information — requires conversion to 3D for docking.
STRING database
A database of known and predicted protein-protein interactions used to construct protein interaction networks in network pharmacology studies.
SwissADME
A free web tool developed by the Molecular Modeling Group at the Swiss Institute of Bioinformatics for predicting pharmacokinetics, drug-likeness, and medicinal chemistry properties of small molecules.
SwissTargetPrediction
A web server that predicts the most probable molecular targets of a compound based on its structure. Used in the first step of network pharmacology to identify compound targets.
Torsion tree
In a ligand PDBQT file, the hierarchical representation of the ligand's rotatable bonds. Defined by ROOT, BRANCH, and ENDBRANCH records. Essential for Vina to know which bonds to rotate during sampling.
TPSA (Topological Polar Surface Area)
The sum of surface areas of polar atoms (O, N, and their attached H). TPSA <140 Å² is associated with good oral absorption; <60 Å² correlates with blood-brain barrier penetration.
UniProt
A comprehensive protein sequence and functional information database. Used to look up protein identity, function, disease associations, and post-translational modifications for target characterization.
Van der Waals radius
The effective radius of an atom accounting for its electron cloud. Two atoms are in van der Waals contact when their centers are approximately the sum of their radii apart. Closer than this = steric clash.
Virtual screening
The computational process of docking a large library of compounds against a target and ranking them by predicted binding score, to identify hit compounds for experimental testing without synthesizing or purchasing all compounds.
Vinardo
An alternative scoring function for AutoDock Vina 1.2+. Activated with scoring = vinardo in conf.txt. May outperform the default Vina scoring function for certain target classes.
Water-mediated interaction
An indirect interaction between a ligand and protein mediated by one or more bridging water molecules. Important in many binding sites but difficult to predict computationally in rigid docking.
X-ray crystallography
The experimental method by which most PDB structures are determined. A protein crystal is exposed to X-rays, producing a diffraction pattern from which atomic positions are calculated by Fourier transform methods.
ZINC database
A free database of commercially available compounds in ready-to-dock formats. Provides PDBQT-formatted ligands for virtual screening at zinc.docking.org.
